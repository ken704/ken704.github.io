CASE STUDY

Superstore Profitability Analysis

Tools: Excel (PivotTables) · Power BI (interactive dashboard)

Data: the public “Sample Superstore” retail dataset from Kaggle, covering 9,993 order lines with sales, discount and profit across US regions and product categories. It is practice data, not a real company’s records.

Swipe or use the arrows below to move through the analysis →

THE QUESTION

Where does this retailer make money, and where does it lose it?

  • Which product categories and sub-categories are profitable, and which lose money?
  • Does heavier discounting hurt profit?
  • Which region is weakest on profit, not just on sales?
APPROACH

How I got there

  • Loaded and checked the data, then grouped the discounts into four bands: 0%, 1-20%, 21-40% and over 40%
  • Built Excel PivotTables for category, sub-category, discount band, region and customer segment, calculating margin as total profit ÷ total sales
  • Built a Power BI dashboard with KPI cards, three charts and a Region filter, using a consistent palette: navy for normal results, red only for losses
KEY FINDING 1

Discounts above 20% turn profit into loss

29.5%margin at no discount
11.9%at 1-20% off
-15.3%at 21-40% off
-77.4%at over 40% off

Orders discounted above 20% make up only about 16% of sales, yet they lost roughly $135K combined, against $286K of total profit.

KEY FINDING 2

Furniture sells a lot but barely earns

2.5%Furniture margin on $742K sales
17.0%Office Supplies
17.4%Technology

Inside Furniture, Tables (-8.6%, about -$17.7K) and Bookcases (-3.0%, about -$3.5K) lose money. Supplies, in Office Supplies, also runs a small loss (about -$1.2K).

KEY FINDING 3

Heavy discounting appears to explain the weak spots

  • About 44% of Table sales carry a discount above 20%. Without those orders, Tables would have earned about $13K instead of losing $17.7K.
  • The Central region has the lowest margin (7.9%, against 14.9% in the West) and the highest average discount (about 24%, against about 11% in the West).
  • Customer segment matters little: margins range only from 11.5% to 14.0%.

This shows an association, not proof of cause. Heavily discounted products may also have been weak sellers to begin with.

RECOMMENDATION

What I’d suggest

  • Set a discount ceiling. Margin turns negative above 20%, so discounts beyond that point could need manager approval. As an illustration, not a forecast: if those orders had only broken even, total profit would have been roughly 47% higher.
  • Review Tables and Bookcases. Check their pricing and how often they are discounted, since they account for most of Furniture’s weak margin.
  • Look into the Central region’s discounting. It discounts the most and earns the least.
  • Protect the strong lines, such as Copiers (37.2% margin), Accessories (25.1%) and Paper (43.4%).
NOTES ON THE DATA

Caveats worth knowing

  • This version of the dataset has no order date, so no trend over time could be analysed.
  • There is no Order ID, so 17 fully identical rows were kept: they can’t be proven to be errors and may be genuine repeat orders.
  • Margin is total profit ÷ total sales, not an average of row-level margins.
  • The dashboard’s chart headlines describe the full dataset and do not update when the Region filter is used.
